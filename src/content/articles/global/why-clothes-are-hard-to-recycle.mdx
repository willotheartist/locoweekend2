The image of circular fashion is wonderfully simple: an old shirt becomes material for a new shirt, which becomes another one when it wears out. It preserves the pleasure of buying while promising that the material never really leaves the story.

The industrial reality is more demanding. Someone must collect the garment, identify what it contains, separate it from unsuitable material, prepare it for a particular process and find a buyer for the output. Each stage has its own costs and failure points. A collection scheme starts that chain; it does not demonstrate that the chain has been completed.

**Clothes are difficult to recycle into new clothes because fibre mixtures, trims, finishes and damage make the input inconsistent, while recycling processes need suitable material and a viable market for the result.** The European Commission's textiles strategy identifies fibre blending and substances that complicate recycling as design problems. It specifically discusses polyester-cotton mixtures and the difficulties created by elastane. [EU Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles, section 2.1](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52022DC0141).

## First, what does “recycled” mean here?

Reusing a shirt means another person wears it, or its current owner keeps using it. Recycling means processing its material into another product. Turning it into a cleaning cloth, recovering fibre for a new textile and remaking it into another garment are different outcomes.

Those distinctions matter because an announcement about “clothes collected” can sound like an announcement about “new clothes made from old clothes”. The quantities are not interchangeable. Nor is a product containing some recycled material automatically evidence that the product itself will be recycled again.

The European Commission's textiles-strategy overview states that only about 1% of material in clothing is recycled into new clothing. Read that as a claim about the clothing-to-clothing loop, rather than a claim that no other reuse or material recovery occurs. [European Commission's textiles strategy](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/strategy/textiles-strategy_en).

| Term | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collected | An item entered a collection system | Its final destination |
| Reused | The existing item is used again | An unlimited future lifespan |
| Recycled content | Some input material came from a recycling process | That the input was an old garment |
| Textile-to-textile recycling | Textile material became textile material again | That every collected item followed that route |
| Recyclable | A suitable recycling process may exist | That an accessible system will actually use it |

A useful environmental claim tells you which of these achievements it is describing. A vague one lets the reader supply the most reassuring interpretation.

## The label inside a T-shirt is an engineering brief

A cotton-polyester blend is not simply a pile of cotton next to a pile of polyester. The fibres are combined in a textile built to behave as a useful material. Stretch, handle, appearance and durability can all be reasons for combining components in clothing.

The difficulty appears when a recycler needs a particular feedstock. The product was designed to work as an assembly; the recycling process may need to undo that assembly or tolerate its composition. What was convenient at the design stage can become costly at the recovery stage.

The Commission's 2022 strategy notes that blends are difficult to separate and that elastane can interfere with many textile-recycling technologies. That does not establish that every blended garment is impossible to recycle. It establishes why a generic promise should be replaced with a process-specific question: who can handle this composition, at what scale and with what output? [European Commission on textile design and recycling](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52022DC0141).

Consider an imaginary jacket with a woven outer layer, a different lining, padding, a zip and printed decoration. Calling it “a polyester jacket” may be useful when shopping. It is an incomplete description of the object someone must process. The recycling system has to deal with the item that exists, not the simplified version on the front of the hangtag.

## Shredding a garment does not restore virgin fibre

Mechanical recycling of cotton can involve sorting material, breaking it down and opening it into fibre. Cotton Incorporated's CottonWorks resource explains that this process can reduce fibre length and affect strength, which is one reason recycled cotton is commonly blended with other fibres for subsequent use. It also distinguishes relatively consistent pre-consumer material from the more varied post-consumer supply. [CottonWorks: recycled cotton](https://cottonworks.com/cotton-sustainability/recycled-cotton/).

This is a less cinematic problem than a warehouse full of discarded clothes, but it is central to the economics. A factory needs an output that can meet a buyer's specification. Recovering something fibrous is not the same as recovering a material suitable for every yarn or garment.

That does not make mechanical recycling a failure. It means the appropriate test is what it produces and what that output replaces. A process can have a useful role without turning every old item into an identical new one.

It also explains why a recycled-content percentage should not be read as a complete quality verdict. A fabric is an engineered product. The relevant questions include how it performs, how long the finished garment remains useful and what material inputs were required to achieve that result.

## The sorting problem comes before the clever machine

Imagine being asked to cook a consistent meal from hundreds of sealed bags whose ingredients change daily. Before improving the cooker, you need to know what has arrived. Textile recycling has its own version of that problem: the machinery needs a usable and reasonably predictable input.

Collection quality makes a difference. The European Environment Agency describes contamination risks in collection systems, including damp textiles and mould, and notes that narrow margins can make washing or drying unsuitable material uneconomic. A bag that keeps clothing clean and dry therefore serves a practical purpose beyond making the donation look presentable. [EEA: management of used and waste textiles](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/management-of-used-and-waste-textiles-in-europes-circular-economy).

A public collection point also needs clear instructions. Does it accept wearable clothes only, damaged textiles, shoes or household linen? Those categories can have different destinations. A system that asks for one category should not be treated as a universal disposal route for another.

From the household's perspective, the best action starts with the collector's actual rules. From the system's perspective, good communication is part of the infrastructure. Sorting becomes harder when people have been encouraged to believe that anything fabric-shaped can go through the same slot.

## A recycled polyester jumper may have started as a bottle

Recycled polyester is frequently presented through the language of circular fashion. Yet recycled input can come from another product category. Textile Exchange's Materials Market Report 2024 says most recycled polyester still came from PET bottles, and describes the limitations of textile-to-textile recycling. [Textile Exchange's 2024 materials report](https://textileexchange.org/knowledge-center/reports/materials-market-report-2024/).

There is nothing logically contradictory about a textile containing recycled plastic from packaging. The problem arises when the claim encourages the buyer to imagine a closed clothing loop that has not been demonstrated.

Ask two separate questions. What was the input before it became this fabric? What is the credible next route for the finished garment? An answer to the first does not answer the second.

This is a useful discipline across product categories. Recycled material is information about part of a product's history. Recyclability concerns a possible future. Actual recycling requires that future to happen. Combining all three into the word *circular* can hide more than it explains.

A better label would tell you the percentage, the material and the source category in plain language. It would also distinguish the fabric composition from any trims or components excluded from that claim. Precision makes a modest achievement easier to trust.

## Why collecting more is only part of the solution

The EEA estimated that the EU generated 6.95 million tonnes of textile waste in 2020, approximately 16kg per person. It warned that without sufficient sorting and recycling capacity, more separately collected textiles could still be incinerated, landfilled or exported. These are dated estimates and a system-level warning, not a forecast of what will happen to any particular bag. [EEA's assessment of textile waste and collection capacity](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/management-of-used-and-waste-textiles-in-europes-circular-economy).

The logic is straightforward. Increasing the amount delivered to the beginning of a chain does not automatically increase the capacity at its narrowest point. A collection target can be met while the desired final outcome remains constrained elsewhere.

A serious programme therefore needs evidence from the whole route. Who sorts the items? Which fractions go to reuse, which to material recovery, and which cannot be used? Does reporting describe destinations at the first transfer or verified final treatment?

These questions are not an argument against collection. They are how collection becomes accountable. Households cannot personally follow every garment through an industrial supply chain. The organisations making claims on their behalf need to provide that information in a form people can understand.

## Export is a destination, not an outcome

An exported garment may be used again. It may also move through further sorting and trading before reaching its final destination. The word *exported* tells you that a border was crossed, not whether an item was ultimately worn, recycled or discarded.

This distinction matters when reading a sustainability report. A tonne transferred to another organisation has left one company's possession. That is not necessarily the same as a tonne successfully reused. The chain of responsibility should not disappear at the same moment as the shipment.

For consumers, the practical question is whether the collection provider can describe its downstream arrangements and the limits of its knowledge. Absolute promises deserve scrutiny when the underlying system contains multiple businesses and jurisdictions.

An export figure becomes more useful when it comes with information about the receiving operation and its results. A shipment receipt establishes a transfer; evidence that the garments found another wearer establishes something more. The difference deserves to remain visible in the reporting.

## Fashion can design a longer first life

Recycling has an appealing quality for a business built around selling new things: it begins after the customer has finished with the old thing. Durability asks an earlier question. Could the customer remain satisfied with the existing garment for longer?

LocoWeekend's [The Gorpcore Plateau](/articles/the-gorpcore-plateau) looks at what happens when practical outdoor clothing becomes an aesthetic. The recycling question brings that tension back to the object itself. A product can communicate ruggedness without giving its buyer clear information about repair or future material recovery.

A longer first life is not only about resistance to physical wear. Fit, comfort and the ability to repair ordinary damage matter to whether someone continues choosing an item. A coat kept indefinitely but never worn is occupying space, not delivering the same service as a coat used through another winter.

This is where design becomes more interesting than a recycling symbol. Can a damaged fastening be replaced? Can the construction be repaired without dismantling the whole garment? Does the buyer understand how to look after it? These are concrete questions a brand can answer before purchase.

Our examination of [how long a smartphone should last](/business/how-long-should-a-smartphone-last) makes a related point: a replaceable part can preserve a useful whole. The engineering is different, but the commercial choice is recognisable. A product's future should be part of what the seller explains today.

## How to read a clothing take-back offer

Begin with the promise itself. Is the company offering to collect unwanted items, sort them for possible reuse or guarantee a particular recycling route? Those are different services. The most ambitious wording requires the strongest evidence.

Then look for results rather than only participation totals. The number of bags received is easy to communicate. The proportions successfully reused, recycled into textiles, recovered for other purposes or disposed of are more informative about the programme's performance.

Check the reporting boundary. Does “our clothes” mean the brand's own products or everything it accepts? Does “recycled” include production scraps that never reached a customer? Is the figure for a particular year, facility or global operation? A percentage without its denominator is an invitation to misunderstand.

Finally, consider any incentive attached to the scheme. A voucher can make participation convenient, but its value to you depends on whether you were going to buy something anyway. Handing over an unwanted shirt does not create a need for a new one. Treat the collection decision and the purchasing decision separately.

## What to do with the clothes you already have

Start with condition and usefulness. A wearable item may have a more valuable next life as clothing than as raw material. If you want to pass it on, use a route that accepts its condition and category. If it is damaged, check repair options or a collection route explicitly accepting damaged textiles.

Keep items dry and follow the provider's packaging instructions. Do not assume the local household recycling bin accepts clothing simply because clothing can, in some circumstances, be recycled. The relevant question is what that local system is equipped to collect.

Before buying a replacement, ask whether you are solving a real clothing need or responding to a reassuring disposal story. A difficult recycling system does not require perfect consumer behaviour. It does require greater honesty from businesses about what happens after the collection photograph has been taken.

The most convincing version of circular fashion would be visible in ordinary details: garments worth keeping, repairs worth doing, collection rules that make sense and reporting that follows the material beyond the shop. The bag through the slot would still feel satisfying. It would also be the beginning of an account someone could finish.
